Guide · Feel + Move

Similar symptoms. Different causes. Sometimes more than one.

Numbness, odd leg sensations, pain, restless sleep and difficulty moving can come from very different parts of the nervous system. Some conditions look alike, some occur together, and one can change while another stays. This guide shows how clinicians tell them apart, and helps you bring better questions to your appointment.

Safety first · before anything else on this page

Some symptoms can't wait.

If any of these are happening, don't use this page. Get medical help straight away. These apply whatever diagnosis you already have.[10][16]

000Emergency, Australia 1800 022 222healthdirect, if you're unsure

Now · call 000 or go to an emergency department

  • Sudden weakness or numbness of the face, arm or leg, especially on one side
  • Sudden trouble speaking, understanding, or seeing
  • Weakness or difficulty walking that is getting worse over hours to days
  • New bladder or bowel problems together with back or leg symptoms
  • New numbness around the groin, buttocks or inner thighs
  • Trouble breathing or swallowing

Soon · see your GP promptly

  • Weakness or clumsiness that is slowly getting worse
  • Numbness or tingling that keeps spreading
  • Blurred or lost vision in one eye over days, with or without pain when you move it

Adapted from NICE NG127 and NSW Health emergency referral criteria. Wording awaiting Australian GP and neurology review.

Where it may come from · localise before labelling

Clinicians ask where in the nervous system first.

The same word, such as tingling, can come from the brain, the spinal cord, a nerve root, nerves throughout the body, one trapped nerve, or a sleep-related pattern. Pick a pattern to see what makes a clinician think of it, what it doesn't establish, and who usually assesses it.

Who assesses it

A description isn't a location. What you feel is only half the picture. An examination can find signs a diary or questionnaire can't, which is why only a clinician can say where a problem sits. Patterns overlap, and exceptions are common.
Three possibilities · keep them all open

Look-alike, side by side, or making each other worse.

The restless legs guide uses the same three ideas. They apply across neurology: when two conditions are in play, it's rarely a simple either/or.[5]

A look-alike

A symptom sounds like one condition but comes from another pathway. The fix is to treat what's actually there.

For example: foot tingling after an ankle injury may point to a local nerve, even if it first sounded like restless legs.

Well recognised[6]

Two at once

Two conditions are both present, and each brings its own symptoms. Finding one doesn't mean the other is gone.

For example: MS and restless legs, diabetes-related nerve damage and restless legs, or a central condition plus a trapped nerve.

Association documented[2]

One makes the other worse

An injury, pain, poor sleep or an irritated nerve might worsen another problem that exists on its own. This is an idea to test, not a shared cause.

Why it matters: if one symptom improves, that doesn't mean the other condition has been reversed.

Hypothesis

This page can't tell you which of these applies to you. Only an assessment over time can, and the answer can change as new findings come in.

Evidence · what's known and what isn't

Misdiagnosis happens in both directions.

MS is sometimes diagnosed when it isn't there, and sometimes missed when it is. Both cause harm. The numbers below justify careful assessment, not a new theory of disease.

From a 2022 review and meta-analysis. It shows the two often occur together. It can't show that one causes the other.[2]

Established

MS is sometimes misdiagnosed, and sometimes missed.

Different study populations give very different estimates.[1][3]

Established

Several conditions can mimic MS clinically or on MRI.

They include migraine and vascular changes in the brain's white matter, NMOSD and MOG-associated disease. Telling them apart is specialist work.[3][4]

Established

A trapped nerve can cause sensations mistaken for restless legs.

That's why the restless legs criteria include a fifth: nothing else better explains the symptoms.[6]

Association

Restless legs is more common in people with MS.

28% versus 8%, with wide variation between studies and possible confounding.[2]

Association

Nerve damage and restless legs often occur together.

Supported by pooled observational studies, summarised in the restless legs guide.[5]

Early only

Releasing nerves in the leg improves true restless legs.

Small studies without comparison groups reported improvement. They can't show the surgery works, and no guideline recommends it.[5][7]

Hypothesis

An irritated nerve could worsen restless legs that exist independently.

Worth testing. Not demonstrated.

Unsupported

Treating a trapped nerve reverses MS, or explains brain and spinal cord findings.

No good evidence supports this. A trapped nerve at the ankle can't explain, for example, changes in the optic nerve or lesions in the brain.

How MS is diagnosed now

The 2024 McDonald criteria, published 2025

The current criteria add the optic nerve as a place where MS can be shown, and allow supporting evidence where appropriate.[8]

  • The central vein sign and paramagnetic rim lesions on MRI
  • Kappa free light chains in spinal fluid
  • All interpreted by a specialist, alongside history, examination and a search for other explanations[9]

No symptom checklist, including this one, can stand in for these criteria.

Questions · for your clinician

Six questions that keep the picture open.

They don't ask for a particular diagnosis or test. They ask how the whole picture fits together, and what would change it.

  1. What do the examination findings point to?Brain, spinal cord, nerve roots, the nerves themselves, or a pattern like restless legs.
  2. Which of my findings are still unexplained?So nothing quietly drops out of the picture.
  3. Could there be two problems happening at once?And would each need its own plan?
  4. What would make you change the working diagnosis?A new symptom, a result, or a lack of response to treatment.
  5. When should we review it?A date or a trigger, so there's a clear point to look again.
  6. Who should I contact if things change before then?And which changes mean I shouldn't wait.
Early research · kept separate on purpose

What's being explored, and what isn't on offer.

Nerve surgery and restless legs

Small studies, no comparison groups

Two reports from one clinic found lower restless legs scores after releasing nerves in the lower leg. Without a comparison group, the change can't be separated from placebo or natural ups and downs.[5]

This guide doesn't recommend surgery. No guideline endorses it for restless legs, and there is no evidence it changes MS. Never stop or change MS treatment on the strength of a nerve finding without your neurologist.[7]

Details and a chart of the 2026 study are in the restless legs guide.

H1Finding what's also there

In people with a central diagnosis and troublesome leg sensations, could structured recording of restless legs and nerve symptoms help clinicians confirm additional problems? Measured by clinician-confirmed diagnoses, not website scores.

H2Each symptom on its own track

When nerve damage and restless legs are both established, does treating the nerve change local pain while restless legs scores change differently?

H3Keeping the unexplained visible

Could an "explained, partly explained, unexplained" list improve second-opinion notes? Measured by usefulness to neurologists, not by how many diagnoses change, because over- and under-diagnosis both harm.

These are research questions that need ethics approval, clinician co-design and independent review before any result is claimed.
