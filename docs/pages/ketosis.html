Move · Ketosis

Ketosis is a real fuel switch.
The brain-boost claims outrun the evidence.

Go long enough without carbohydrate and your liver starts turning fat into ketones, a second fuel your brain and muscles can run on. That part is settled physiology. The claims that ketones are a superior fuel, sharpen thinking or burn fat faster rest on much thinner evidence. This guide explains the switch, shows what blood ketone numbers mean, and grades the claims.

01 · The fuel switch

Run low on carbs and your liver makes a second fuel.

After a meal, insulin is high and you run mostly on glucose. As the hours pass without carbohydrate, insulin falls, the liver’s glucose store (glycogen) runs down, and fat is released from storage. The liver turns some of that fat into ketones: mostly beta-hydroxybutyrate (BHB) and acetoacetate, plus a little acetone that you breathe out.[1] Researchers call the changeover the metabolic switch. It typically happens 12 to 36 hours after your last meal.[2] Drag through it below.

The brain takes what the blood offers.

Ketones cross from blood into the brain on carrier proteins, and the brain uses them in step with the blood level.[3][4] So the share of the brain’s energy that comes from ketones depends on how high they are.

After an overnight fast
<5% of brain energy

Blood ketones are usually under 0.5 mmol/L and barely register as brain fuel.[3]

At 1.5 mmol/L
~18% of brain energy

Pooled human measurements, at a level inside the ketogenic-diet range.[4]

Weeks into a fast
60%+ of brain energy

In people studied after 5 to 6 weeks of supervised starvation, ketones had replaced glucose as the brain’s main fuel.[5][1]

02 · Reading the numbers

Ketosis is not ketoacidosis.

Both mean ketones in the blood. The difference is how much, and whether insulin is still in charge. In nutritional ketosis, insulin keeps ketone production in check and the blood stays at a normal pH. In diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA) there isn’t enough insulin, ketones climb unchecked and the blood turns acidic. DKA is a medical emergency.[6] Slide the reading to see what a number usually means.

How doctors define DKA

All three, not ketones alone.

  1. Diabetes, or blood glucose of 11.1 mmol/L or more
  2. Ketones of 3.0 mmol/L or more
  3. Acidic blood: pH under 7.3, or bicarbonate under 18 mmol/L

03 · Three on-screen notes

Three on-screen notes, checked.

This guide started with an episode of The Diary Of A CEO about keto, where short notes labelled “Community Notes” appear on screen as the guest talks.[14] Three of them are about ketosis. Two hold up. The third wraps a true statement around a number that doesn’t mean what it appears to.

Note 1 · The diet
The note, in shortKeto is high fat and low carb, so the body burns fat instead of carbohydrate. It’s used for weight loss and may help conditions such as epilepsy.
  • The definition is right. A ketogenic diet means 20 to 50 g of carbohydrate a day, under 10% of energy.[7]
  • Epilepsy is the strongest use, not a maybe. Children with drug-resistant epilepsy were about 3 times as likely to become seizure-free as with usual care.[15]
  • Burning fat isn’t the same as losing more of it. With calories held equal in a metabolic ward, a ketogenic diet didn’t speed up body-fat loss.[16]

Holds upAnd undersells epilepsy.

Note 2 · Fasting
The note, in shortIntermittent fasting can bring brief, mild ketosis even on a high-carb diet. It fades faster than on low carb, because carbs raise insulin and cut ketones.
  • The switch starts around 12 hours without food, so longer daily fasts do reach it.[2] In one trial an 18-hour daily fast raised morning ketones.[17]
  • Eating flips it back. On an 18-hour daily fast the switch is on for about six hours, until the next meal.[2]
  • “Mild” is doing the work. After an overnight fast, ketones are usually under 0.5 mmol/L, the bottom of the nutritional ketosis range.[3]

Holds upAs long as you read “mild” literally.

Note 3 · The brain
The note, in shortKetones give 25–30% more ATP per molecule than glucose and cross into the brain easily. Many people report sharper focus in ketosis.
  • Per molecule, glucose gives more. About 30 ATP from one glucose against about 21.5 from one BHB, by standard textbook accounting.[18]
  • They do reach the brain. Carrier proteins move them across, and uptake rises with the blood level.[3]
  • People report it. A trial didn’t find it. Three weeks of ketosis changed nothing on thinking tests in 11 healthy adults.[19]

Half rightTrue on transport, wrong on the number.

Where “25–30%” comes fromIn 1995, researchers pumped glucose through isolated rat hearts, then added ketones. The hearts did about 25% more work for the oxygen they used.[20] A later paper counted 13 ATP from BHB against 10 from pyruvate, the half-molecule that glucose is split into.[21] Both compare fuels per unit of oxygen or per fragment, in rat hearts. Neither says a ketone molecule carries more energy than a glucose molecule, and neither was measured in a human brain.

04 · The evidence

What holds up, and what doesn’t yet.

One thing to know before the grades: most keto research is in people with epilepsy, type 2 diabetes or obesity, and most of it is short. Very little tests healthy adults looking for an edge.

The largest review, 2023

68 trials. Four findings rated high quality.

An umbrella review pooled 17 meta-analyses covering 68 randomised trials. It found 51 statistically significant effects, and graded the evidence behind each one.[22]

Only four were backed by high-quality evidence: fewer seizures in children with drug-resistant epilepsy, lower triglycerides at 3 and at 12 months in adults with type 2 diabetes, and higher LDL cholesterol at 12 months in the same group.

05 · Check first

Check these before fasting or cutting carbs.

For most healthy adults a spell of mild ketosis is low risk. For the people below it can be dangerous, or it needs medicines adjusted first.[7]

Type 1 diabetes

Without enough insulin, ketones can climb unchecked into ketoacidosis.[8] Fasting and very low carb eating both push ketones up, so they need your diabetes team involved from the start.[7]

SGLT2 inhibitors

Dapagliflozin, empagliflozin and other medicines ending in -gliflozin carry a small but definite risk of ketoacidosis, sometimes with near-normal blood glucose.[11] Fasting and low-carb diets raise that risk.[30][6] Talk to your prescriber before either.

Insulin or sulfonylureas

Cutting carbs lowers blood glucose quickly. Doses usually have to come down first, or you risk hypos. That’s a decision for your GP or diabetes team, made before you start.[7]

Pregnant or breastfeeding

Listed as a reason for caution in Australian Diabetes Society guidance on very low carb diets.[7] Get individual advice before restricting carbs or fasting.

An eating disorder, past or present

Strict food rules and fasting windows can feed disordered eating, and it’s listed as a caution in the same guidance.[7] Butterfly’s National Helpline is free and confidential, 8am to midnight, 7 days.

Butterfly 1800 33 4673

Get help now if

You’re very thirsty and passing a lot of urine, with vomiting, stomach pain, fast breathing, breath that smells like nail polish remover, or drowsiness and confusion. These are signs of ketoacidosis.[6] Call triple zero.

Emergency 000

Also ruled out, or needing specialist advice: end-stage liver failure, rare inherited disorders of fat or ketone metabolism, and pancreatic disease that limits insulin.[7] If you’ve had kidney stones, raise that with your GP too.[29]

Use it for what it’s good at

A real state, with a narrow set of proven uses.

Ketosis is normal physiology. A ketogenic diet is a proven therapy for hard-to-treat epilepsy and a recognised option for type 2 diabetes. For a healthy adult chasing sharper thinking, the evidence isn’t there yet.
