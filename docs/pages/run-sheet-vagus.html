Your body's brake runs through one nerve.

The vagus nerve runs from your brainstem to your gut. It slows your heart, runs digestion, damps down inflammation, and carries most of your body's status reports back to the brain. This page covers what it does, how to track it, and which habits the evidence backs.

The route

From brainstem to gut, one wandering nerve

"Vagus" is Latin for wandering. It is the longest cranial nerve and reaches more organs than any other. Top to bottom, it goes here.

  1. Brainstem

    Medulla · jugular foramen

    Starts in four nuclei in the medulla that send and receive its signals. It leaves the skull through the jugular foramen, one nerve on each side.

  2. Ear

    Auricular branch

    Supplies skin in the ear canal. It is why cleaning your ear can make you cough, and why ear-clip stimulators target this spot.

  3. Throat and voice box

    Pharyngeal and laryngeal branches

    Swallowing, the gag reflex and your vocal cords. The left recurrent laryngeal branch loops under the aorta before climbing back to the voice box, so problems in the chest can show up as a hoarse voice.

  4. Neck

    Carotid sheath

    Runs beside the carotid artery and jugular vein. Implanted stimulators wrap the left vagus here, and handheld devices press on the side of the neck.

  5. Heart

    Cardiac branches

    The brake on your heart's pacemaker. Left alone, the pacemaker would run near 100 bpm. Heart rate rises a little on each breath in and falls on each breath out. That swing is vagal, and slow breathing makes it bigger.

  6. Lungs

    Pulmonary branches

    Airway tone, stretch sensing and the cough reflex. Stretch signals from the lungs are part of why breathing pattern changes heart rate.

  7. Stomach, liver and pancreas

    Gastric and hepatic branches · through the oesophageal hiatus

    Relaxes the stomach to receive a meal, drives acid and enzyme release, and reports fullness and nausea to the brain.

  8. Intestines

    Coeliac branches

    Moves food along most of the gut, as far as the colon, and senses what is inside it. Signals from the gut make up most of the traffic in the nerve.

80–90%

Mostly a listening nerve. Most vagal fibres carry information up from the organs to the brain: how full the stomach is, how the gut is doing, how the lungs are stretching. Only 10–20% carry commands down.

The inflammatory reflex

When the brain senses inflammation, vagal signals lead to acetylcholine release near immune cells called macrophages. Acetylcholine binds their α7 nicotinic receptors and they make less TNF, a key inflammatory messenger.

Vagus→Acetylcholine→α7 receptor→Less TNF-α

How to read it

You can't measure the nerve, but you can watch your heart

There is no direct test of "vagal tone" at home. Heart rate variability (HRV) and resting heart rate are indirect markers that most rings, watches and chest straps already record.

What

RMSSD and resting heart rate

RMSSD is the beat-to-beat HRV measure most tied to vagal activity. Resting heart rate works too: lower usually means more vagal braking.

RMSSD in ms · HR in bpm
When

Same conditions, every day

Use the overnight average from a ring or watch, or a 1-minute reading lying down just after waking. Readings taken at random times mostly measure what you were just doing.

What helps

Habits ranked by the evidence

Graded by the strength of the research on vagal markers or related outcomes, strongest first.

PracticeEvidenceWhat the research showsHow to do it

Slow breathing, about 6 breaths a minute

Solid

ResearchA 2022 meta-analysis of 223 studies found vagally mediated HRV rises during slow breathing and stays higher after sessions and training programs.

How5 minutes, once or twice a day. In through the nose for 4 seconds, out for 6. Use the guide above.

Regular aerobic exercise

Solid

ResearchEndurance training lowers resting heart rate and raises resting HRV over weeks to months.

How2.5 to 5 hours a week of moderate activity, the Australian guideline. Brisk walking, cycling or swimming all count.

Less alcohol

Solid

ResearchIn 4,098 Finnish employees, HRV-based recovery in the first 3 hours of sleep fell about 9% after 1–2 drinks, 24% after 2–6 and 39% after more than 6.

HowAlcohol-free evenings. Your next-morning HRV will usually show the difference.

HRV biofeedback

Moderate

Research24 studies, 484 people: a large drop in self-reported stress and anxiety (g = 0.83). The authors call for better-controlled trials.

HowBreathe at your own resonance rate, usually 4.5–7 per minute, with a chest strap or app that shows heart rate rising and falling.

Cold on the face or neck

Moderate

ResearchIn a trial of 71 adults, cold on the side of the neck raised RMSSD and lowered heart rate compared with no cold. The effect is short-term.

HowSplash cold water on your face, or hold a wrapped cold pack on the side of your neck, when you need to settle quickly.

Enough sleep

Moderate

ResearchHRV peaks in deep sleep. Short or broken nights show up as lower overnight readings.

How7 to 9 hours, with a regular lights-out time.

Meditation and yoga

Limited

ResearchStudies are small and mixed. The slow breathing built into many practices is the likely active part.

HowFine to keep if you enjoy it. Pick styles with slow, long exhales.

Humming, chanting, singing

Limited

ResearchSmall studies only. All three stretch out the exhale, which is the likely mechanism.

HowHarmless. Treat it as slow breathing with sound.

Gargling, ear massage, "reset" moves

Unproven

ResearchNo controlled trials show a lasting change in vagal activity.

HowSpend the time on breathing or a walk instead.

Consumer ear-clip stimulators

Early

ResearchEar (auricular) stimulation is being studied for many conditions. Devices sold online for general wellness claim more than the evidence shows.

HowWait for better trials, or use one inside a study or with a clinician.

    Claims vs evidence

    What social media gets wrong

    "Low vagal tone" is something you can diagnose from a wearable.

    Vagal tone isn't directly measurable. HRV is an indirect marker shaped by breathing, fitness, age, medication and the device itself.

    You can reset your vagus nerve in 30 seconds.

    Practices shift your nervous system's balance for minutes to hours. Your baseline moves over weeks of exercise, sleep and less alcohol.

    Polyvagal theory explains how the vagus nerve works.

    It is popular in therapy and online, but a 2023 review in Biological Psychology found each of its five core premises untenable. Its language can help people talk about stress; the physiology behind it is disputed.

    Higher HRV is always better, so compare yours with friends.

    HRV varies widely between healthy people and between devices. Your own trend is what matters.

    When to get checked

    See your GP if you notice

    • !Fainting or near-fainting, especially during exercise or with no clear trigger.
    • !Palpitations, or a slow pulse with dizziness or breathlessness.
    • !A hoarse voice lasting more than 3 weeks.
    • !Trouble swallowing, or food sticking on the way down.
    • !Feeling full after small meals, repeated vomiting, or bloating that doesn't settle.

    Before you start

    Cold plunges and ice baths can trigger a cold-shock response. If you have heart disease, high blood pressure or a history of fainting, check with your GP first.

    Never practise breath-holding in or near water.

    Pregnant, or on heart or blood pressure medication? Ask before starting anything that changes your heart rate on purpose.
