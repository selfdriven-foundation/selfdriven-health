Restless legs, or a trapped nerve?

Both can make your legs feel wrong at night and push you to get up and move. They start in different places and need different fixes. This guide helps you spot which pattern fits, so the right things get checked.

Two problems · the same feeling

One can feel just like the other. They start in different places.

Restless legs syndrome (RLS) comes from the brain and spinal cord, and iron handling in the brain is thought to play a central part. A trapped nerve comes from a single spot where something presses on a nerve in the foot, ankle or leg.[16][10]

Restless legs syndrome

Also called Willis–Ekbom disease
Starts in
The brain and spinal cord. Iron and dopamine signalling are both involved.[16]
Tell-tale sign
Builds when you sit or lie still, eases while you move, and is worse in the evening or at night.[1]
Linked to
Low iron, pregnancy, kidney failure, some medicines, and a family history.[16]
How common
About 3 in 100 adults have moderate to severe symptoms at least twice a week. About 2 in 100 children and teens have RLS.[14][15]

Trapped nerve

Entrapment neuropathy
Starts in
One spot, where a nerve is squeezed by a tight tunnel, swelling, bone, or scar tissue.[10]
Tell-tale sign
Sits in one patch of skin, may bring numbness, and can be set off by pressure, shoes or a position.
Linked to
Injuries, surgery, repeated ankle sprains, swelling and tight footwear.[10]
Scar tissue
A scar can bind a nerve so it can't glide as you move. Not every scar does this, and deep scarring may not show on the skin.
21.5%of people with nerve damage also have RLS
41.8%of people with RLS also have nerve damage

From a 2021 review that pooled many studies. These figures cover nerve damage in general, such as from diabetes, rather than trapped nerves alone. Studies used different criteria, so treat them as a rough guide.[3]

Evidence · how sure we are

What's solid, and what's still a hunch.

The idea that a trapped nerve can drive restless legs has some early support. Most of what helps day to day rests on firmer ground.

Solid

RLS is diagnosed on five criteria, and look-alikes must be ruled out.

The fifth criterion was added in 2014 so that cramps, positional discomfort, arthritis and similar problems stop being labelled RLS.[1]

Solid

Iron matters for RLS.

The 2025 US sleep guideline says to check ferritin and transferrin saturation in people with RLS that needs treatment, and to treat low iron.[9]

Solid

Nerves in the foot and ankle can be trapped.

Injury, scarring, fibrosis, swelling and tight tunnels are recognised causes. Care starts with footwear, orthotics and physio, with surgery kept for when those fail.[10][11]

Moderate

RLS and nerve damage often occur together.

A 2021 review found RLS was more common in people with nerve damage than in people without, and the reverse. It shows a link, and can't show that one causes the other.[3]

Early

Releasing nerves in the lower leg lowered RLS scores in 18 adults.

One clinic, no comparison group, and the surgeon who does the operation led the study. Details below.[5]

Early

An earlier look-back at 42 patients reported less burning, tingling and crawling after the same surgery.

It reviewed past records, used the clinic's own 0–10 symptom scales, and did not use the standard RLS rating scale.[4]

Unclear

Magnesium for restless legs.

A 2019 review found one small trial, which showed no benefit, plus case reports. The 2025 guideline makes no recommendation either way.[13][9]

Unproven

A foot scar can cause true RLS.

No study has tested this directly. The research so far looks at nerve damage in general or at lower-leg surgery, not at foot scars.

Getting checked · in the order it usually goes

Check both. Start with your GP.

In Australia, a GP visit is the first step for restless legs, and blood tests including iron are routine.[17][18] If a podiatrist has raised a nerve problem, look into it alongside the RLS checks, and don't let it replace them.

    What helps · treat what's actually there

    The fix depends on the cause.

    Treatments for a trapped nerve and for RLS are mostly different, which is why sorting the pattern first matters.

    If a nerve is trapped

    Take the pressure off

    First

    Footwear changes, orthotics fitted to unload the nerve, physio, and easing off what flares it. A badly placed orthotic can press on the nerve and make things worse.[10]

    Next

    An image-guided injection around the nerve. Relief from the numbing medicine supports the diagnosis without proving it.

    Last

    Surgery to free the nerve, when entrapment has been shown, symptoms are disabling and other care has failed. For tarsal tunnel surgery, about 3 in 4 had good or excellent results across mostly low-quality studies. Long-standing symptoms and nerve scarring predicted worse results.[11]

    If it's RLS

    Iron, triggers, then medicine

    First

    Correct low iron, and cut back the triggers listed below.[9]

    Medicines for adults

    The 2025 guideline's strongest recommendations are the gabapentinoids, such as gabapentin and pregabalin, and iron by drip when iron is low. Older dopamine medicines (pramipexole, ropinirole, rotigotine) are now advised against for standard use, because over time they can make RLS worse.[9]

    Children

    Iron by mouth, when tests show it's needed, is the only treatment with a guideline recommendation.[9]

    If it's both

    Treat each, one at a time

    Plan

    Treat both problems, changing one thing at a time so you can tell what helped.

    Track

    Keep the diary going. A drop in one area, or in night-time episodes, tells you which treatment is working.

    Medicines

    If you take a dopamine medicine for RLS, don't stop it suddenly. Talk to your doctor about any change.

    Things that can make RLS worse

    AlcoholCaffeineNicotineShort sleepUntreated sleep apnoeaSome sedating antihistaminesSSRI and SNRI antidepressantsDopamine-blocking anti-nausea and antipsychotic medicines

    Don't stop a prescribed medicine on your own. Ask your doctor or pharmacist whether it could be playing a part.[9][16]

    Conditions linked to RLS

    Iron deficiencyPregnancy, especially lateKidney failure and dialysisNerve damage

    They are worth checking for, because treating them can ease RLS.[16]

    Children · and when to get help fast

    Children describe it differently.

    The same two patterns apply, but the words, the checks and the treatment change.

    • Use their words. Ask your child to describe the feeling and write down exactly what they say. The children's criteria are built around the child's own description.[2]
    • It isn't rare. About 2 in 100 children aged 8 to 17 have RLS.[15]
    • A scar alone proves nothing. A past injury or scar doesn't by itself mean a nerve is damaged.
    • Treatment differs from adults. Adult medicine recommendations don't carry over. Iron is the one treatment with a recommendation for children, and only when tests show it's needed.[9]
    • Ask for the right specialist. A paediatric neurologist, or a foot and ankle specialist who sees children, can help when symptoms persist.

    Get help fast

    Now · call 000 or go to an emergency department

    • Weakness in a leg that comes on suddenly or worsens over hours to days
    • New trouble walking
    • Numbness or weakness together with new bladder or bowel problems

    Soon · see your GP promptly

    • Weakness that is slowly getting worse
    • Numbness or tingling that keeps spreading

    For anyone, child or adult. Adapted from the UK's NICE guidance for children with suspected neurological conditions.[19]

    Questions · for your next appointment

    Seven questions to take with you.

    They aim the next tests at a specific nerve and a specific cause, rather than scanning everything.

    1. Which nerve could be involved, and where along it?Ankle, arch, forefoot, top of the foot, or higher up the leg.
    2. What makes you think scar tissue is pressing on it?The exam, my injury history, or something on a scan.
    3. Would an ultrasound of that area help?It can show a nerve near the surface and the scar beside it.
    4. Do my symptoms fit RLS, a nerve problem, or both?And should I see a neurologist or sleep specialist?
    5. Can we check my ferritin and transferrin saturation?Ideally a morning test, with no iron supplements the day before.
    6. Could any of my medicines be making this worse?Including antihistamines, antidepressants and anti-nausea medicines.
    7. What do we try first, and when do we judge whether it's working?So there's a clear point to review.
