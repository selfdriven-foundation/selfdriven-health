Move · Walking

7,000 steps does most of the work.

Across 57 studies, people taking 7,000 steps a day had a 47% lower risk of dying during follow-up than people taking 2,000, and the gains flatten from there. Walking also keeps back pain away for longer, blunts blood-sugar spikes after meals and lifts mood. Here is what the evidence supports, how brisk is brisk, and a planner for your week.

01 · The dose

Most of the gain comes before 7,000 steps.

A 2025 review in The Lancet Public Health pooled studies that counted people’s steps with a device and followed them for years. Against a low 2,000 a day, here is how much lower the risk was at 7,000.[1]

These are links, not proof. People who walk more may already be healthier in other ways, and the studies adjust for that only in part. For death, heart disease, dementia and falls, the curve was steep at first and flattened between about 5,000 and 7,000 steps. Rows backed by two or three studies are less certain than the first.[1]

More steps past that point added little to survival, which matches the wider review’s 5,000 to 7,000.[2][1]

1965
The year 10,000 entered the language

It likely comes from Manpo-kei, a Japanese pedometer whose name means “10,000 steps meter”. A sales name, not a research finding.[2]

02 · What it does

Seven things a walk does, and how sure we are of each.

The strongest evidence comes from trials that put people on a walking plan and compared them with people who weren’t. The rest comes from following large groups over time. Each row says which.

Back pain

Trial-proven

The WalkBack trial took 701 Australians who had just recovered from low back pain. Half got a walking plan built up over six sessions with a physio across six months. They went a median 208 days before pain returned, against 112 days for the group with no programme, and their risk of recurrence was about 28% lower.[7]

Fascia movement guide

Blood sugar

Small trial

In 41 adults with type 2 diabetes, three 10-minute walks after meals lowered the rise in blood sugar after eating by 12% compared with one 30-minute walk at any time. After dinner, the biggest and most carbohydrate-heavy meal, the drop was 22%.[5]

Mood

Moderate, low certainty

Across 218 trials in people with depression, walking or jogging had a moderate effect on symptoms, among the largest of any exercise type. Most of the trials had a high risk of bias, so the certainty is rated low.[6] At 7,000 steps a day, depressive symptoms were 22% less likely.[1]

Depression guide

Heart and lifespan

Strong link

At 7,000 steps a day, death from heart disease or stroke was 47% less likely and new heart disease or stroke 25% less likely than at 2,000.[1] Pace adds to it: in 50,000 British walkers, those who called their usual pace average had a 20% lower risk of death than slow walkers, and brisk walkers 24%.[3]

Brain

Early link

Dementia was 38% less likely at 7,000 steps than at 2,000, but that estimate rests on only two studies.[1]

Knees

Link

In 1,212 people with knee osteoarthritis, those who walked for exercise had about 40% lower odds of developing frequent knee pain. Walking did not wear the joint out faster.[8]

Falls

Add balance

People who step more fall less, by 28% at 7,000 steps.[1] But in trials, programmes built around walking alone have uncertain effects on falls. Balance and functional exercises cut the rate of falls by 24% across 39 trials, so pair the walk with them.[9]

03 · Pace

Brisk means about 100 steps a minute.

A walk counts as moderate activity, the kind the guidelines ask for, from about 100 steps a minute. That is 25 steps in 15 seconds, which you can check without a watch.[4]

No beat handy? Brisk is a pace that leaves you slightly out of breath or sweaty if you keep it up. For many people that is 5 to 6 km an hour.[3]

Steps first, pace second

Volume

Total steps carry most of the effect

In the 16,741 older women, step intensity stopped mattering once total steps were counted.[2] If brisk feels like too much, walk more at an easy pace.

Intensity

Brisk minutes count twice

They add steps and they count toward the 150 minutes of moderate activity a week in the Australian guidelines. Self-rated brisk walkers also had the lowest risk of death in the British data.[10][3]

04 · Your week

Plan a walking week.

Australia’s guidelines ask adults aged 18 to 64 for 150 to 300 minutes of moderate activity a week, plus strength work on two days.[10] For people 65 and over, the ask is at least 30 minutes on most, preferably all, days.[11] Set your usual steps, add walks and see where your walking days land.

05 · The evidence

What the evidence supports, and two things it doesn’t.

Graded from trial-proven to myth. A link from a long-running study is still worth acting on when the habit is free and safe, but it is not the same as a trial.

Trial-proven
A walking programme keeps low back pain away for longer.
WalkBack: 701 adults, six physio sessions over six months. Median 208 days to recurrence against 112, hazard ratio 0.72. It cost about AU$7,800 per quality-adjusted life year gained.[7]
Small trial
Short walks after meals blunt blood-sugar spikes.
41 adults with type 2 diabetes, two weeks on each plan. Three 10-minute walks after meals beat one 30-minute walk by 12% on post-meal blood sugar, and by 22% after dinner.[5]
Moderate
Walking eases depression.
218 trials, 14,170 people with depression. Walking or jogging: Hedges’ g −0.62 against active controls, a moderate effect. Certainty is low because most trials had a high risk of bias.[6]
Strong link
About 7,000 steps a day goes with longer life and less heart disease.
57 studies. Death from any cause 47% lower than at 2,000 steps, across 14 studies. Observational, so some of the gap may come from healthier people walking more.[1]
Limited
Walking helps arthritic knees rather than harming them.
1,212 people with knee osteoarthritis followed for years. Walkers had about 40% lower odds of new frequent knee pain. Observational.[8]
Uncertain
Walking on its own prevents falls.
A Cochrane review could not tell whether walking programmes reduce falls. Balance and functional exercise did: rate of falls 24% lower across 39 trials and 7,920 people.[9]
Myth
You need 10,000 steps for walking to count.
The number likely comes from a 1965 pedometer’s name. In older women, benefit levelled off at about 7,500, and in the wider review between 5,000 and 7,000.[2][1]
Myth
Only 30 minutes in one go counts.
Three 10-minute walks after meals did better for blood sugar than one 30-minute walk.[5] The guidance for people 65 and over suggests starting with 10 minutes once or twice a day.[11]
06 · Check first

Before you step it up.

Walking is about as safe as exercise gets, which is why it’s the default advice. A few situations still need care.

Chest pain on a walk

Stop. Chest pain, arm or shoulder pain, or shortness of breath can be a heart attack. Some people feel sick, sweaty or dizzy, or have pain in the back, neck or jaw, and these are more common in women. A heart attack is a medical emergency, so call Triple Zero rather than trying to walk it off.[13]

000Copied

A health condition or new to exercise

If you have a heart or lung condition or diabetes, take medicines that affect heart rate or blood sugar, or haven’t been active for a while, talk to your GP before stepping up. Build gradually. The guidance for people 65 and over starts at 10 minutes once or twice a day and moves to 15 minutes twice a day after two weeks.[11]

Sun and heat

Check the UV index before you go and use sun protection whenever it is 3 or above.[14] On hot days, walk early or late, carry water and shorten the walk.

Sore joints or unsteady on your feet

Start on flat, even ground in supportive shoes. Pain that gets worse from one walk to the next, or new swelling, is a reason to see a GP or physio. If you are unsteady, add balance exercises, which are what cut falls in trials.[9]

Start tonight

Ten minutes after dinner.

It’s the smallest step on this page with a trial behind it. In people with type 2 diabetes, an after-dinner walk cut the rise in blood sugar by 22%. Five nights a week is 50 minutes, a third of the way to 150.[5]
