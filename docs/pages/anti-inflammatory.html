Five bowls that turn the heat down.

Chronic, low-grade inflammation hums in the background of heart disease and type 2 diabetes. No single food switches it off. A Mediterranean-style pattern lowers it a little, and in a large trial cut heart attacks and strokes by about 30%. Here are five bowls built on that pattern, in Australian measures, with a week planner and a shopping list.

01 · The heat

A short burst heals. A low hum that never switches off is the problem.

A cut, a cold or a hard training session sets off acute inflammation: heat, swelling, then repair. Chronic low-grade inflammation is quieter and lasts for years. Food is one lever on it, alongside waist size, smoking, sleep and movement.

Anti-inflammatory eating aims only at the right-hand column, and it works through the whole pattern rather than single foods. Researchers even score diets with a Dietary Inflammatory Index built from 45 foods and nutrients.[3]

04 · The evidence

What the evidence supports.

Graded from solid to myth. Small, real effects are still worth having when they come from meals you would eat anyway.

Solid
A Mediterranean-style pattern cuts heart attacks and strokes.
PREDIMED: 7,447 adults at high risk, followed for a median 4.8 years. Hazard ratios 0.69 with extra virgin olive oil and 0.72 with nuts, against a reduced-fat diet. About 1 fewer event per 100 people.[2]
Guideline
Eat 2–3 serves of fish a week, including oily fish.
Heart Foundation: a serve is 100 g cooked, about the size of your palm. Aim for 250–500 mg of marine omega-3 (EPA and DHA) a day, plus 1 g of plant omega-3 (ALA).[4]
Moderate
The same pattern lowers inflammation markers, a little.
33 trials, 3,476 adults: hs-CRP −0.39 mg/L, IL-6 −0.16 pg/mL. CRP results varied widely between trials, and TNF-α didn’t move.[1]
Limited
Beans and fibre lower CRP.
Linked in population studies. In trials, non-soy legumes moved CRP by −0.21 mg/L, which was not significant, and a 2024 review in adults with overweight found no effect on CRP, IL-6 or TNF-α. Eat them for fibre, protein and low salt.[10][11]
Limited
Berries calm inflammation.
A 2020 review of 32 trials found small drops, mostly with concentrated anthocyanin doses above 300 mg a day. Newer reviews find no clear effect from usual intakes.[12][13]
Limited
Extra virgin olive oil works like ibuprofen.
Its oleocanthal blocks the same COX enzymes in the lab. The researchers estimated 50 g of oil, about 2¾ Australian tablespoons, matches roughly 10% of an adult ibuprofen dose. It has never been tested as pain relief.[17]
Association
Ultra-processed food drives inflammation.
A 2025 review of 24 studies: more ultra-processed food went with higher CRP in 11 of 17 adult analyses and higher IL-6 in 5 of 6. Observational, so cause isn’t proven.[18]
Association
Eat 30 different plants a week.
From the American Gut Project: people eating 30+ plant types a week had more diverse gut microbes than those eating 10 or fewer. A rule of thumb, not a tested target. All five bowls together reach 17.[19]
Myth
Nightshades cause inflammation.
Arthritis Australia: there is no proof that tomatoes, potatoes, eggplant or capsicum affect arthritis symptoms. Keep the cherry tomatoes.[16]
Myth
Dairy is inflammatory.
Trial reviews find dairy neutral to slightly anti-inflammatory, except in people with a cow’s milk allergy. One of the reviews had a dairy-council author.[14][15]
05 · Check first

Check these before you change how you eat.

General guidance, not a meal plan for you. If any of these apply, the bowls may need changes, and a GP or Accredited Practising Dietitian can tailor them.

Pregnant or planning

Cook eggs until the white is firm and the yolk has started to set, so boil the soft-boiled eggs in bowls 1 and 2 for 8 minutes or more.[8] Canned tuna and salmon are fine. Limit shark (flake) and billfish to one 150 g serve a fortnight with no other fish that fortnight.[7]

Watching salt

Australia’s sodium target is 2,000 mg a day; average intake is about 3,600 mg.[20] Feta, olives, canned fish and packet roasted chickpeas carry most of the salt here. Pick springwater tuna, rinse olives and chickpeas, and use feta or olives rather than both.

On warfarin

Spinach and other leafy greens are high in vitamin K, which affects warfarin. You don’t need to avoid them, but keep the amount steady from week to week and tell your doctor before a big change.

Kidney disease

Avocado, tomato, sweet potato, legumes and yoghurt are high in potassium. If you’ve been told to limit potassium or phosphorus, ask your renal dietitian before making these bowls a habit.

Allergies

The bowls contain fish, egg and milk. Swap fish or chicken for more chickpeas or firm tofu, and leave out the feta or yoghurt. With a cow’s milk allergy, dairy is inflammatory for you.[15]

An inflammatory condition

With arthritis, IBD or psoriasis, food supports treatment and doesn’t replace it. High-fibre, legume-heavy bowls may not suit an IBD flare. Find an Accredited Practising Dietitian at Dietitians Australia.
