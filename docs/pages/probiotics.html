The right microbe for the right job.

Probiotics help with a few specific problems, and only when the strain was tested for that problem. Here is where the evidence holds, how to read a label, and how to run a fair trial on yourself.

01 · What counts

A probiotic is one named microbe, tested for one job.

The agreed definition is live microbes that, taken in a large enough amount, give a health benefit.[1] All three parts matter. It has to be alive when you take it, there has to be enough of it, and the benefit has to be shown in people.

LacticaseibacillusGenus rhamnosusSpecies GGStrain

Genus

The family name. In 2020 the old Lactobacillus genus was split into 25, so L. rhamnosus and Lacticaseibacillus rhamnosus are the same microbe under an old and a new name.[2]

Species

Like a first name. Many strains share it, and some effects are common to the species, mostly general gut support.[1]

Strain

The ID that links a product to its trials. This one is GG, also filed as ATCC 53103. The strongest results on this page come from named strains.

Its four cousins

These words turn up on the same shelf. Only the first one promises live microbes with a proven effect.[3]

This page

Probiotic

Live microbes that, in a large enough amount, give a health benefit.

A capsule of one named strain

Fermented food

Food made by microbes growing in it. It may or may not still hold live ones.

Yoghurt, kefir, kimchi, sauerkraut

Prebiotic

Food your gut microbes use in a way that gives you a health benefit.

Inulin, galacto-oligosaccharides

Synbiotic

Live microbes plus food they use, together giving a benefit.

A probiotic with added inulin

Postbiotic

Dead microbes or their parts, prepared so that they give a benefit.

Heat-killed bacteria

In Australia, yoghurt must carry at least 1 million live cultures per gram, and its one approved live-culture claim is easier lactose digestion.[4] Live cultures alone don’t make a food a probiotic.

The four you’ll see on labels

Lactobacilli

Now 25 genera, from Lactobacillus to Limosilactobacillus
Named strains
L. rhamnosus GG; L. reuteri DSM 17938
Best evidence
Antibiotic diarrhoea in children at high doses, and colic in breastfed babies.[10][22]

Bifidobacteria

Named for their split, Y-shaped cells
Named strains
B. animalis subsp. lactis; B. longum subsp. infantis
Best evidence
B. lactis adds bowel movements in constipation. Mixes help very premature babies, in hospital only.[19][23]

S. boulardii

Saccharomyces boulardii, a yeast, so antibiotics don’t kill it
Best evidence
Antibiotic diarrhoea and C. difficile prevention.[8][10]
Watch
Not with antifungal medicines or a central line.[13][33]

S. thermophilus

Streptococcus thermophilus, grows in chains
Where you meet it
A yoghurt starter culture, and part of one named 4-strain mix.[8]
Best evidence
With L. bulgaricus, it helps people who struggle with lactose digest yoghurt.[4]

04 · The evidence

What the evidence supports.

Graded from moderate to not shown. Nothing here reaches solid, because trials mix strains, doses and people, and many are small.

Moderate
High doses prevent antibiotic diarrhoea in children.
33 trials, 6,352 children: diarrhoea fell from 19% to 8%. At 5 billion CFU a day or more, it fell from 23% to 8%.[10]
Moderate
Probiotics prevent antibiotic diarrhoea in adults.
42 trials, 11,305 adults: risk down by about a third, with no benefit where the risk was low. In 2,941 inpatients aged 65 and over, no difference.[5][6]
Moderate
Probiotics protect very premature babies.
60 trials, 11,156 infants: fewer deaths (moderate certainty) and less necrotising enterocolitis, a serious bowel disease (low certainty). No clear effect in the most premature. Hospital use only.[23]
Moderate
B. lactis helps constipation.
It adds bowel movements a week, without softening stools or easing pain and bloating.[19]
Limited
Probiotics halve C. difficile diarrhoea.
38 trials, 13,179 people on antibiotics: 3.2% to 1.6%, low certainty. Worth it only where the risk is moderate to high.[12]
Limited
One strain eases colic in breastfed babies.
L. reuteri DSM 17938, 4 trials, 345 infants: about 25 minutes less crying a day by day 21. Not enough data in formula-fed babies.[22]
Limited
Probiotics ease IBS.
The British guideline says they may help, with very low quality evidence. The American College of Gastroenterology suggests against them.[17][18]
Limited
Probiotics prevent colds.
24 trials, 6,950 people: fewer had a cold (low certainty), and fewer needed antibiotics (moderate). Many different strains.[26]
Limited
Probiotics lift mood.
34 trials: a small effect on depression that shrank after allowing for unpublished studies, and a very small one on anxiety.[27]
Not shown
Probiotics treat gastro in children.
Two trials in 943 and 886 children found no difference in how sick they got or how long diarrhoea lasted.[21]
Not shown
Probiotics restore your gut after antibiotics.
In 21 adults, an 11-strain probiotic delayed the gut’s return to normal for months. One small study of one product.[15]
Not shown
Probiotics help you lose weight.
15 trials, 957 people with overweight: 0.6 kg over 3 to 12 weeks.[28]
Not shown
Probiotics prevent eczema and allergy.
Australia’s allergy society doesn’t recommend them for this. Results conflict and the best strain and dose are unknown.[25]
Myth
An AUST L number means it works.
Listed medicines use ingredients pre-approved as low risk. The TGA doesn’t assess whether the product works before it goes on sale.[29]
Myth
More strains make a better probiotic.
Guidelines name exact strains and mixes because effects depend on the strain. A strain count on the front is not evidence.[8]
05 · Trial it fairly

Run a 12-week trial on yourself.

For IBS and constipation, the honest answer is to try one and see. A weekly score makes that test fair, so you keep what works and stop paying for what doesn’t.

  1. One product at a time

    Write down the full strain name. If it works, that’s the one to buy again.

  2. Score the week before

    Your week-0 score is the baseline everything else is compared with.

  3. Change nothing else

    Same dose, same diet. Two changes at once and you can’t tell which one worked.

  4. Stop at 12 weeks if it hasn’t helped

    The British IBS guideline says to try one for up to 12 weeks, then stop if you’re not better.[17]

This is an example log. Press Start my own to clear it.

Week 0 is the week before you start. Score each week: 0 means none, 10 means the worst it gets. Lower is better.

We call a score 30% or more below your baseline “clearly better”. That’s our rule of thumb, not a medical cut-off. Your log saves on this device only.

06 · Check first

Who should skip them, and when to wait.

Probiotics are safe for most healthy people. Gas and bloating are the usual side effects.[31] These are the exceptions.

Weak immune system

healthdirect advises against probiotics for people on chemotherapy or immune-suppressing drugs, transplant recipients, people with HIV, and people who have just had surgery.[31]

Central line or intensive care

In a review of 59 blood infections with this yeast, 55 patients had a central line, and some caught it from capsules opened nearby.[13] In one children’s ICU, the capsule strain of L. rhamnosus reached the blood of 6 of 522 patients.[14]

Severe pancreatitis

In a trial of 296 patients with predicted severe acute pancreatitis, 16% died on a probiotic mix against 6% on placebo, and 9 had bowel ischaemia against none.[32]

Premature babies

In 2023 a premature baby died of sepsis from the bacterium in a probiotic product. The US FDA has approved no probiotic as a drug for infants.[24] Only the neonatal team decides.

On antifungal medicine

S. boulardii is a yeast, and its product information says not to take it with antifungals.[33] A bacterial strain is the logical alternative. That’s our inference, so ask your pharmacist.

Timing with antibiotics

Start within 2 days of the first antibiotic dose: later starts showed no clear benefit against C. difficile.[7] Leave about 2 hours between the antibiotic and a bacterial probiotic. That’s practical advice, not tested.[9]

See a GP, whatever you’re taking

A probiotic doesn’t fix these. Get them checked.

  • Watery diarrhoea three or more times a day during or after antibiotics, especially with fever or tummy pain
  • A fever while taking a probiotic, if you have a central line or a weak immune system (urgent)
  • A child with gastro who is drowsy, has fewer wet nappies, or can’t keep fluids down
  • Blood in your poo, or black poo
  • A change in bowel habit that lasts, or weight loss you can’t explain

Three questions for your pharmacist

  1. Which strain is in it, by its full name?
  2. How many CFU a day, guaranteed to the expiry date?
  3. Was this strain tested for my problem?
