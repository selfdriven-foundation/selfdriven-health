The vagus nerve · what it does

A two-way line between your brain and your organs.

The vagus is the tenth cranial nerve. It runs from the brainstem through the neck and chest into the abdomen, which is why it has been called the wanderer, and it is the main component of the parasympathetic nervous system.[6] Most of its traffic runs upward: about 80% of its fibres carry signals from the body to the brain.[7]

Heart

Heart rate

It supplies the heart's main parasympathetic input and acts as a brake on its pacemaker. The beat-to-beat give this creates is what heart rate variability (HRV) measures.[6][10]

Gut

Digestion

It reaches much of the digestive tract and reports on its state to the brain, making it a main route of the gut–brain axis.[6][7]

Brainstem

Stress recovery

As the main parasympathetic nerve, it helps bring heart rate and arousal back down once a stressor has passed.[6]

Immune response

Inflammatory responses

Both its sensory and motor fibres have anti-inflammatory effects. Treatments that raise vagal tone can reduce cytokine production.[6][7]

Lungs & vessels

Breathing and cardiovascular reflexes

It carries stretch and pressure signals from the lungs, heart and major vessels to the brainstem, which uses them to adjust breathing, heart rate and blood pressure.[6][9]

Why it matters

Change the signal, change the state.

Because so much traffic runs from body to brain, changing what your body does changes what your brain hears. Slowing your breathing is the clearest example, and it is the book's starting point.

Six levers · natural ways to support vagal activity

Six everyday ways in, and how strong each one is.

The benefit column is the plain-language claim. The evidence column says what the research actually shows, and whether it measured the vagus itself or a broader outcome.

Activity

What the evidence says
Slow, controlled breathing
BenefitStress regulation
Strong

A review of 223 studies found slow breathing raised vagally mediated HRV during a session, straight after it, and after multi-week programmes.[8]

Humming and singing
BenefitRespiratory regulation
Early

Singing slows your breathing, and in a small choir study singers' heart rates rose and fell together with the phrasing.[10] Humming raised nasal nitric oxide 15-fold in ten men, but that is a sinus effect, not a vagal one.[11]

Regular exercise
BenefitAutonomic resilience
Good

A meta-analysis found training increased cardiac parasympathetic activity in sedentary, healthy adults.[12]

Meditation
BenefitEmotional regulation
Mixed

Across 19 trials, seated mindfulness and meditation did not clearly raise resting vagal HRV.[13] It may still help how you feel, but probably not mainly through this nerve.

Positive social connection
BenefitReduced stress
Strong for health

Across 148 studies, people with stronger social relationships were 50% more likely to survive over the follow-up period.[14] The vagal part of that story is less well measured.

Consistent sleep
BenefitNervous system recovery
Good

In 11 trials, sleep deprivation lowered RMSSD, a vagal HRV marker, and tipped the balance toward the sympathetic side.[15]

Try it · a five-minute exercise

Five minutes of slower breathing.

Slow breathing is the best-supported tool in the book, and you can start now. Press start and follow the circle.

Ready4·6in · out

Left 5:00Breaths 0

  1. Get comfortable

    Sit or lie down. Let your shoulders and jaw soften.

  2. In through the nose for 4

    A gentle, easy breath. No need to fill up completely.

  3. Out slowly for 6

    Through your nose or softly pursed lips, like fogging a window.

  4. Repeat for 5 minutes

    Repeat comfortably for 5 minutes, without forcing your breathing. If 4 and 6 feels like a strain, switch to 3 and 5.

Why this pace? 4 in and 6 out is six breaths a minute, close to the rate at which heart rate and breathing fall into sync.[9] If you feel light-headed or short of breath, stop and breathe normally.

The science · is it legitimate?

Yes, although “reset” oversimplifies the biology.

The core idea holds up well. Some of the theory the book is built on does not, and you can use the first without accepting the second.

Holds up
223studies

Slow breathing shifts vagal activity.

A 2022 systematic review found voluntary slow breathing increased vagally mediated HRV, an indicator of parasympathetic regulation of the heart. The authors call it a low-tech, low-cost technique with few adverse effects expected.[8]

Contested
39experts

Polyvagal theory is under heavy fire.

Psychophysiologist Paul Grossman challenged its five core premises in 2023.[16] In 2026, he and 38 other specialists in vagal physiology and evolution called the theory untenable. They argue, for example, that the dorsal vagus mainly acts on the gut, not the heart, and that respiratory HRV is only a rough index of vagal activity.[17][19] Stephen Porges, who developed the theory, has replied that the critique misrepresents it.[18]

“

Why “reset” oversimplifies

Vagal activity isn't a switch you reset once. It moves with every breath and shifts with sleep, fitness, stress and illness. The lasting changes in studies come from practice repeated over weeks.[8][15] Even the theory's lead critic, Paul Grossman, says dismissing somatic therapies “is not the point”.[19]

Worth reading? Yes, as an accessible introduction to nervous system self-regulation and the feedback loops between brain and body. Treat it as a practical wellbeing guide, not a proven cure for trauma or chronic illness.

Working through trauma? These tools can support professional care but shouldn't replace it. Blue Knot Helpline (complex trauma) 1300 657 380 · Beyond Blue 1300 22 4636 · Lifeline 13 11 14, 24/7. In an emergency, call 000.[20]
