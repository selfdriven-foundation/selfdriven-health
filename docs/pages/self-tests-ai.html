Guide · AI and your test results

Ask AI what it means. Ask your GP what to do.

You can paste a blood, stool or DNA report into an AI chatbot and get a plain-English explanation in seconds. Used well, that helps you understand your report and bring better questions to your GP. Used badly, it can falsely reassure you or scare you. This guide shows how to use it well.

Evidence · what the research shows

The model often knows the answer. The conversation is where it goes wrong.

AI models now score highly on medical exams. The trouble starts when a real person, with half the details and a worry, starts typing.

1 in 10

Australians asked ChatGPT a health question in the first half of 2024. Of those, 61% asked at least one higher-risk question, the kind that usually needs a clinician.[8]

94.9% → 34.5%

Tested alone, AI models named the right condition in 94.9% of cases. People using the same models got there under 34.5% of the time, no better than people who didn't use AI.[9]

100%

Asked to warn people off a brand-name drug and onto its generic, which is the same medicine, GPT models went along with it every time.[10]

Where it helps
  • Plain language. On 53 real lab-result questions, GPT-4's answers beat other models and human answers on a Q&A site for relevance, correctness, helpfulness and safety.[11]
  • How you ask matters. In a proof-of-concept study, asking one question at a time and asking the AI to answer as a clinician made answers more accurate.[12]
  • Language barriers. In Australia, people born in non-English-speaking countries were more likely to use ChatGPT for health.[8]
Where it fails
  • Missing details. In 16 of 30 chats the researchers reviewed, people's first message left out information the AI needed.[9]
  • Mixed answers. Models suggested 2.21 conditions per chat on average, and only 34% were right. Two people with similar brain-bleed symptoms got opposite advice.[9]
  • Wrong ranges. In January 2026, Google removed AI Overviews for liver blood test ranges after the Guardian found they ignored age, sex and other factors.[13]

Doctors are split. In a US survey of 1,692 clinicians, about 70% said patients' chatbot use was positive or made no difference to their practice, but about half rarely or never want patients using AI to interpret test results. In one primary care study, 5% to 13% of chatbot answers to 222 patient questions were unsafe, depending on the chatbot.[14]

By test type · blood, stool, DNA

Good at blood panels. Not to be trusted with raw DNA.

An AI explanation is only as good as the test behind it. Here's what it can do with each kind of result, and what to watch for.

Result
Use AI for
Watch for
Blood panelFull blood count, iron, cholesterol, HbA1c, thyroid
Use AI forExplaining what each marker measures, grouping results, and listing questions for your GP.

Use the range on your report. Reference intervals differ between labs, so PTEx says to use only the one from the lab that ran your test.[1] With enough tests, one result just outside the range is likely by chance.[2] Australian reports use SI units, and HbA1c comes in both mmol/mol and %.[15] Many US sources use mg/dL instead, so a cholesterol of 5.2 mmol/L appears there as about 200.

Bowel screening resultThe free program's FIT kit, or a paid FOBT
Use AI forExplaining what a positive or negative result means, and what a colonoscopy involves.

Don't let a chatbot talk you out of follow-up. A positive result isn't a cancer diagnosis, but the program says to see your GP, who will probably refer you for a colonoscopy. Symptoms after a negative result also mean seeing your GP straight away.[16]

Microbiome reportStool mapping of gut bacteria
Use AI forDefining terms such as diversity, and the names of bacteria.

The explanation can't be surer than the test. Experts still call microbiome testing exploratory (see Self-tests). An AI will explain the report as fluently as a blood test, so don't build a diet or supplement plan from it.

Clinical DNA reportFrom an accredited lab, with a genetic counsellor
Use AI forLearning terms like "pathogenic" or "variant of uncertain significance" before your appointment.

Your counsellor interprets it. Your results can reveal information about your blood relatives, who may not want to know.[17] Use AI to prepare questions, not answers.

Raw DNA fileDownloaded from an overseas ancestry kit
Use AI forNothing health-related. Don't upload it.

The data itself is unreliable. When a clinical lab rechecked them, 40% of the variants reported in raw data from direct-to-consumer tests were false positives.[18] An AI reading the file inherits those errors. The file is also about your relatives, and Australian privacy protections don't apply to overseas services.[17]

Has a lab or doctor contacted you about a result? Act on what they told you. Don't ask a chatbot whether it matters. Not sure how urgent it is? Call 1800MEDICARE on 1800 633 422. In an emergency, call 000.[6]
Before you paste · privacy

Take your name off it. Keep the numbers.

An AI needs your results, units and reference intervals. It doesn't need to know who you are. healthdirect advises leaving out your address, Medicare number and full medical history, and reading the tool's privacy policy first.[4]

Take out
  • Name and date of birth. Give your age instead.
  • Address, phone and email.
  • Medicare, lab, episode and request numbers.
  • Your doctors' names and the clinic's details.
Keep in
  • Each result with its units, reference interval and any H or L flag.
  • Age and sex, because many ranges depend on them.
  • Why you were tested, and whether you were fasting.
  • Medicines and supplements you take.

Australia's privacy regulator goes further. The OAIC recommends that organisations, as best practice, don't put personal information, and particularly sensitive information, into publicly available AI chatbots.[19] That advice is written for businesses, but the reasoning applies to you too.

Check these settings before you paste.

Four widely used assistants, listed alphabetically. Each lets you stop your chats being used for training. In Australia, none of them can connect to your medical records.

TrainingTurn off Improve the model for everyone under Settings, then Data controls. Temporary chats aren't used to improve its models. Rating an answer can still send the whole conversation for training after you opt out.[23]

ChatGPT Health is a separate space whose chats aren't used to train OpenAI's foundation models. Connecting medical records works only in the US.[20] It launched to all US adults in July 2026.[21] In Australia, it started as a limited rollout with a waitlist in January 2026.[22]

Connectors for health records and lab results are for Pro and Max subscribers in the US only.[24] Anthropic says it doesn't use health data from them to train its models.[25]

Copilot Health is rolling out in phases in the US, starting March 2026.[29] Microsoft says information in Copilot Health isn't used for model training.[28]

GeminiGoogle
TrainingFrom November 2026, training has its own setting, Chat content including text & attachments. It's on by default unless you'd already turned off Keep Activity.[31] Human reviewers read some chats.[32] Temporary Chats aren't used to train Google's models.[33]

Google Health Coach, built with Gemini, has been available in Australia since May 2026 with Google Health Premium, at A$14.99 a month. It launched first for Fitbit and Pixel Watch users.[34] Syncing medical records works only in the US.[35]

Not a medical device. The TGA regulates AI as a medical device when it's meant for diagnosis, monitoring or treatment, and says a product with no medical purpose or claim is unlikely to be one.[36] OpenAI, for example, says ChatGPT Health isn't intended for diagnosis or treatment.[20] Settings change often. Before you act · six checks

    
Six checks before you trust an answer.

    
These work with any assistant, including ones not on this page.


    

      
    
        
  1. Did it use your report's numbers?
    Reference intervals differ between labs. PTEx says to use only the one on your own report.[1]
    2. 
        
  2. Did you tell it enough?
    In the Oxford study, people's first message often left out what the AI needed.[9] Age, sex, medicines and why you were tested all change the answer.
    3. 
        
  3. Did you lead it?
    Models tend to go along with what a question assumes, even when they know better.[10] Ask "what does this mean?", not "this is fine, isn't it?"
    4. 
        
  4. Does an Australian source agree?
    Look the marker up on Pathology Tests Explained, which is written and reviewed by pathologists.[1]
    5. 
        
  5. Does it hold up a second time?
    Ask again in a new chat, or ask a different assistant. Patient advocates suggest checking one AI's answer against another.[12]
    6. 
        
  6. Have you told your GP?
    Bring your questions, and say you used AI so your GP can correct anything it got wrong.[14]
