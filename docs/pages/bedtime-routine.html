Nine minutes to wind down.

    Minutes 1–3NowBest evidence

    Slow belly breathing

    Lie on your back with your knees bent or a pillow under them. Rest one hand on your belly. Breathe in through your nose for 4 seconds so your belly rises, then out for 8 seconds, through your mouth or nose.

    If it's a strainSwitch to 4 in · 6 out. Both paces sit in the slow range the research tested. If you feel light-headed, go back to normal breathing.

    Minutes 4–6NowGo gently

    Gentle twist

    Bend your right knee and let it fall across to the left. Right arm out, and look right if your neck is comfortable. Stop where you feel a stretch, not pain. Keep breathing slowly, and swap sides every 45 seconds.

    Easier versionKeep both knees bent and together, and let them drop slowly to one side, then the other.

    Minutes 7–9NowFollow the bowel

    Belly massage

    Flat hand, comfortable pressure. Start low on your right side, slide up to your ribs, across under them, and down your left side. Repeat in slow loops.

    Why this directionThe loop follows your large bowel, the way NHS constipation leaflets teach it. Pressure should be firm enough to feel and never painful.[9]

    What to expect · and when

    Some effects show up tonight. Others take weeks.

    Studies of these steps measured different things over different lengths of time. Here's what they found, in the order you'd notice it.

    Tonight

    Your body shifts down a gear

    Heart rate variability rises while you breathe slowly, a sign the vagus nerve is more active. A review of 223 studies found the rise during a session and just after it.[1] A longer out-breath also feels more relaxing.[2]

    Solid
    About 4 weeks

    Bowels, and how you feel you slept

    In small trials, regular belly massage eased constipation within four weeks.[7][8] People who practised slow breathing before bed for about a month said they slept better, though sleep-lab results were mixed.[4]

    Massage: moderateSleep: limited
    About 8 weeks

    Stress hormones

    In one trial, 40 healthy adults did 20 sessions of slow belly breathing over 8 weeks. Afterwards their saliva cortisol was lower and they reported fewer negative feelings.[3] It's one small study, so treat it as a promising lead.

    Limited
    A common mix-up

    The belly massage isn't lymphatic drainage.

    Lymphatic drainage is a light-touch therapy for lymphoedema. It just moves the skin and usually starts at the neck.[10] Lymph moves mostly because the vessels pump it themselves.[12] Lymphoedema researchers at Macquarie University say people without swelling probably don't need drainage.[11] The massage here is for your bowel.

    Keep expectations right

    A wind-down routine isn't an insomnia treatment.

    It can help you relax before sleep. If you have trouble sleeping most nights, the recommended first treatment is cognitive behavioural therapy for insomnia (CBT-I).[19][18]

    When to see your GP
    Evidence · graded

    Where each piece of the routine stands.

    From what's well shown to what hasn't been shown at all.

    Solid

    Slow breathing raises heart rate variability

    Seen during and straight after a session across 223 studies.[1] A longer out-breath also felt more relaxing in a 30-person lab study.[2]

    Moderate

    Belly massage eases constipation

    In small randomised trials, constipation severity fell 28% to 55% more than in comparison groups. A US family-medicine review rates it a B.[7][8]

    Moderate

    A regular yoga practice improves sleep quality

    Across 19 trials in women, yoga improved self-rated sleep quality, but not insomnia severity. These tested whole classes, not one pose.[6]

    Limited

    Slow breathing before bed improves sleep

    People report sleeping better after weeks of practice. Objective measures are mixed and most studies are small, such as one of 14 people with insomnia.[4][5]

    Limited

    Slow breathing practice lowers cortisol

    One trial of 40 adults found lower saliva cortisol after 8 weeks of training. It hasn't been repeated at scale.[3]

    Limited

    Deep breathing helps move lymph

    Slow, deep breaths raised lymph flow in a study of dogs. In people, the lymph vessels' own pumping does most of the work.[13][12]

    Not shown

    A spinal twist on its own improves sleep

    We found no study of this pose and sleep. It's in the routine as a comfortable stretch.

    Not shown

    Lymphatic drainage helps people without swelling

    Australian lymphoedema experts say healthy people probably don't need it. Even in lymphoedema, adding drainage to compression gave only a modest extra benefit in small trials.[11][14]

    Cautions · skip or change a step

    Skip or change a step if any of these apply.

    The routine is low risk for most people. These are the exceptions.

    Minutes 1–3

    Breathing

    • You feel light-headed or tingly. Go back to your normal breathing.
    • Lying flat makes you breathless. Raise your head and shoulders, and mention it to your GP.
    Minutes 4–6

    Twist

    • You have osteoporosis. Twisting movements are on the list to avoid. Skip this step and keep breathing instead.[15]
    • You've had a hip replacement. Hospital advice is no bending the hip past 90°, crossing your legs or twisting the leg for at least six weeks. Ask your surgeon or physio first.[16]
    • Pain shoots down a leg, or your back hurts more. Stop.
    Minutes 7–9

    Belly massage

    • Skip it if you're pregnant, have inflammatory bowel disease or an unstable spinal injury, or have a belly wound less than six weeks old.[9]
    • Reflux or a hiatus hernia. Avoid long upward strokes toward the ribs. One NHS leaflet turns its opening stroke downward for this.[9]
    • New, severe or unexplained belly pain. See your GP instead of massaging it.
    Sleep · when nine minutes isn't enough

    Still not sleeping? Ask your GP about CBT-I.

    Australian GP guidance names cognitive behavioural therapy for insomnia (CBT-I) as the first treatment for ongoing insomnia. Breathing and relaxation are one part of it.[19][18]

    First treatment

    Cognitive behavioural therapy for insomnia

    A structured course, usually 4 to 8 sessions a week or a fortnight apart, with a psychologist, in a group or through a digital program. The Sleep Health Foundation says it helps up to 80% of people with insomnia.[18]

    Sleep restriction
    Time in bed is matched to the sleep you're actually getting, then widened.
    Stimulus control
    The bed is for sleep. If you're awake after 15 to 20 minutes, you get up.
    Relaxation
    Mindful breathing among others, done in bed only if it takes under 15 minutes. Nine minutes fits.
    Rethinking sleep
    Working through worries and beliefs that keep you awake.

    See your GP if

    • Poor sleep is affecting your days, or you often wake up unrefreshed.[17]
    • Your mood is low or you're anxious as well.[17]
    • You feel restless in bed. Restless legs can cause insomnia; our restless legs guide helps you describe it.[17]
    • You snore heavily, or someone notices you stop breathing in your sleep. Sleep apnoea needs its own check.[19]
